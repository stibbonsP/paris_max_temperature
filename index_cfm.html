Forecast of maximal annual temperature in Paris

What is the probability that we reach 50°C in Paris in 10 years? Or before the end of the century?

We will try to provide a quantitative answer.

Why do we care?

Impact on our health:

  • Additional stress on our bodies
  • Lower recovery at night

Impact on our ability to work:

  • Disruption in public transports
  • School closure

Impact on our operations:

  • Electricity supply in Paris may be at risk
  • Our datacenters may have to shutdown

Defining Temperature

We take as reference the temperature recorded by the Meteo France station in Parc Montsouris

The Urban Heat Island effect is particularly pronounced in Paris. There is a strong temperature gradient towards the center of the city.

The temperature measured in the streets can be much higher than the one recorded in Montsouris.

Illustration of the Urban Heat Island effect

Our approach

How to estimate probabilities of future temperature records?

  • We estimate the statistical properties of daily temperatures
  • We generate random processes mimicking the behavior of future temperatures
  • We deduce probability density functions for observables of interests, for instance the probability to reach a given temperature threshold
  • The standard approach is to rely on Global Climate Models simulations
  • These can be downscaled to the regional scale
  • We can then look for extreme events in the resulting simulation

Heat wave simulations from Viguié et al.

Why are we looking for an alternative?

  • Each simulation can take several months on powerful computers. This is a lengthy and expensive process.
  • We may need many simulations to infer density probability functions.
  • Parametrizing, running and interpreting these simulations require sharp expertise in climate models.

The data-driven approach we explore is much quicker and cheaper.

It easily provides probability density functions instead of individual realizations.

To build synthetic temperature data, we combine three components:

  • A trend representing the impact of global warming
  • A seasonal pattern
  • Random fluctuations

The global warming trend

We estimate that temperatures in Paris increase at a rate of \(\approx\) 7°C per century, almost 3 times faster than the world average

=

+

+

First we need a fit for global warming

  • We download historical data (ourworldindata.org)
  • We fit with the following functional form: $$y = \frac{1}{2} \left( a(t - t_0) + \sqrt{a^2(t - t_0)^2 + r^2} \right) $$
  • We find \(a \approx 0.025\)°C / year, \(t_0 \approx 1975\), \(r \approx 0.7\)

Is it realistic to assume linear warming until the end of the century?

  • This scenario lies in-between the "intermediate" and "high" scenarios of the IPCC
  • The "intermediate" scenario feels quite optimistic given the current GHG emission reduction rate

IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report

The next step is to fit the warming rate in Paris compared to global warming

  • We perform the linear regression:
    $$ \min_{c,\ T_0} \left(\mathrm{Paris\ temperature} - c \left(\mathrm{Global\ Warming}\right) - T_0 \right)^2 $$
  • We find \(c \approx \) 2.8, \(T_0 \approx 14.4\)°C

The factor 2.8 we fitted is surprisingly high.

To challenge our result, we repeat this fit with different data: the ERA5 temperatures for many locations in France.

  • On average, warming is twice faster in France than the world average, with significant geographic variations
  • Around Paris, we obtain a factor close to 2

So, which value should we trust between 2 and 2.8?

  • Large cities warm faster than their neighborhing countryside, by a factor \(\approx 1.3\) (Liu, Z., Zhan, W., Bechtel, B. et al)
  • The methodology used to build the ERA5 data makes it rather blind to the Urban Heat Island effect, which is responsible for this increased warming rate

$$ 2 \times 1.3 = 2.6 $$

  • The Urban Heat Island effect effect is particularly strong in Paris

$$ 2 \times \left(1.3 + \mathrm{Paris\ bonus}\right) \approx 2.8? $$

All in all, this makes the factor 2.8 rather plausible

The seasonal pattern

This is simply modeled by a combination of sinusoidal functions

=

+

+

We fit the seasonal pattern with two cosine functions (first two Fourier modes) of periods 1 year and 6 months

  • We perform the linear regression: $$ \min_{A_1,\ A_2,\ t_1,\ t_2} \left(\tilde{T} - A_1 \cos\left(\frac{t-t_1}{1\ \mathrm{year}}\right) - A_2 \cos\left(\frac{t-t_2}{6\ \mathrm{months}}\right) \right)^2 $$ where \(\tilde{T}\) is the Paris temperature minus the previously fitted global warming
  • We find \(A_1 \approx 9.4\)°C and \(A_2 \approx 0.9\)°C

We check on the ERA5 data that this two-mode seasonal fit generally captures most of the seasonal pattern

The residual noise

It is surprisingly well modeled by an auto-regressive process

=

+

+

The difference between the data and the (global warming + seasonal) fit is the residual

The distribution of residuals is well appproximated by a Gaussian with standard deviation \(\approx\) 3.9°C

This remains true for all ERA5 temperature time series, with some dispertion in the standard deviations.

Notice that the left-hand side of the distribution appears to be slightly distorted.

Since we mostly care about temperature extremes, the fit of the right tail is the most important for us.

Somewhat unexpectedly (for us), the magnitude of temperature fluctuations is hardly affected by global warming.

We estimate that the std of the residual increased by \(\approx\) 5% over 75 years.

The auto-correlation of the residual is reasonably well fitted with a single exponential.

The timescale is \(\approx 3.5\) days.

Can this method really capture the increase of extreme event induced by global warming?

We can reproduce the extreme temperature events statistics obtained from GCM simulations.

These are ruled by the shift of the mean much more than by a change of the shape of the distribution.

We generate synthetic temperature time series. For the residual, we chose a standard deviation of 3°C plus 3.5% per degree of warming.

These values a slightly different from the one fitted for Paris. It is not surprising that we need to tune these parameters since the IPCC statistics relies on worldwide simulations.

Conclusions

  • Estimates of future temperature probabilities are useful and not easy to access
  • Our quant toolbox is relevant. Temperatures are more Gaussian than prices.
  • "More extreme temperature events" essentially come from a shift of mean...
  • ...but the mean moves fast & the distribution is narrow!
